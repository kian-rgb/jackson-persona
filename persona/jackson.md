# Jackson

The synthetic ag-lending loan officer we write for.

---

## Who he is

- **Role:** Loan officer at an ag-lender.
- **Experience:** 15+ years originating loans. Rotated through a small community bank, a mid-size regional ag lender, and a Farm Credit institution over his career.
- **Domain knowledge:** Deep. Understands lending stages, borrower roles, credit analysis, guarantors, disclosures, closing docs.
- **Tech comfort:** Low. Uses Outlook, a core banking system he learned in the 2010s, and a browser. That's it.
- **Age:** Doesn't matter — this could be a 35-year-old who came up in a small town or a 60-year-old veteran. What matters is the software vocabulary he *hasn't* absorbed.

---

## What he knows

He knows what a **borrower**, **co-borrower**, **guarantor**, and **collaborator** are. He knows what **pre-qualification**, **credit analysis**, and **closing** are. He can hold a lending conversation with a farmer in one hand and a compliance officer in the other.

He knows lending. He does not know product/software.

---

## What trips him up

Words that make him stop reading and either scroll back, call support, or close the tab:

- Any word that names a UI concept engineers use (**wizard, modal, toolbar, panel, sidebar, breadcrumb, widget, overlay**)
- Any word that describes an action in developer terms (**toggle, threaded, paginated, authenticate, configure**)
- Any adjective that's more about the software's architecture than what it does (**dynamic, reusable, structured, layered**)
- Compound abstract nouns (**data extraction rules, structured data collection**) — he stops at the noun phrase

He doesn't stop because he's dumb. He stops because he has 40 files to work through today and can't spend 30 seconds figuring out what "the sidebar" refers to.

---

## How he reads documentation

- **Skim first, read second.** He scans for the bolded action verbs and numbered steps. If he sees them, he trusts the page.
- **Trusts headings.** If a section header uses a word he doesn't know, he skips it.
- **Follows numbered lists.** He'll do step 1, 2, 3 without reading the paragraph above them.
- **Gives up fast.** Two unknown words in a row and he opens Slack to ask a colleague.

---

## What he wants documentation to do

1. Tell him **what button to click**, in the **right order**.
2. Tell him **what to expect** after he clicks it (so he knows if it worked).
3. Use the **same words the app uses**, exactly.
4. Not explain the platform's architecture. He doesn't care.

---

## The one sentence that captures him

> If Jackson has to Google a word in your documentation, you have failed him.

---

## Why he isn't a real person

Jackson is a composite based on real conversations with ag-lending users, but he is deliberately fictional. This is on purpose:

- **Real users have edge cases** — a specific manager, a specific loan officer, a specific onboarding memory. Composites let the team debate what "Jackson would think" without arguing about one person.
- **Fiction is portable.** Anyone new to the team can meet Jackson in five minutes.
- **Fiction is falsifiable.** If a real user consistently understands things Jackson wouldn't, we update Jackson.

---

## How to use him

When editing or reviewing any user-facing text, ask:

1. Would Jackson know every word here without Googling?
2. Can Jackson find the next step in under 3 seconds of skimming?
3. Does the language match what the app screen shows?
4. Would Jackson need to know *why* this feature exists to use it? (If yes, cut the "why.")

If any answer is "no," edit. The [word-swap table](../framework/word-swaps.md) covers the most common cases.
