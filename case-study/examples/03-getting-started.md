# Example 3: The Getting Started Pages

The lightest-touch example. Three pages, few changes each — but the changes matter because they're the first thing every new user reads.

---

## Why Getting Started is a special case

Every user hits these pages before they've built any product intuition. If Jackson stumbles on his first login page, he doesn't get to page two.

That means the filter here is doing prevention work: fixing the words that would confirm "this is going to be complicated" before Jackson ever sees the actual product.

---

## Page: Platform Overview

**Before (opening paragraph):**
> Landjourney.ai is an all-in-one platform that **streamlines** agricultural lending, **empowering** both lenders and farmers with a modern digital experience. The platform automates repetitive tasks, increases loan volumes, and supports the cross-selling of fintech products — all without disrupting your existing **IT systems**.

**After:**
> Landjourney.ai is an all-in-one platform that **simplifies** agricultural lending for both lenders and farmers. The platform automates repetitive tasks, increases loan volumes, and supports the cross-selling of fintech products — all without disrupting your existing **systems**.

Three fixes:
- `streamlines` → `simplifies` (streamline is marketing vocabulary)
- Cut `empowering both lenders and farmers with a modern digital experience` — the whole phrase adds nothing except adjectives
- `IT systems` → `systems` (Jackson doesn't call it "IT"; his admin does)

**Table row inside the page:**

Before: `Configure reusable request templates, file templates, dynamic forms, signature templates, and document data extraction rules.`
After: `Configure saved request templates, file templates, online forms, signature templates, and document data extraction rules.`

Two swaps in one sentence: `reusable` → `saved`, `dynamic forms` → `online forms`.

---

## Page: Logging In

**Before:**
> 3. Click the **eye icon** to **toggle password visibility** if needed.

**After:**
> 3. Click the **eye icon** to **show or hide your password** if needed.

`toggle password visibility` is a UX-writer sentence. `show or hide your password` is what Jackson does.

**Before:**
> Click the **Google Login** button to **authenticate** using your Google account.

**After:**
> Click the **Google Login** button to **sign in** using your Google account.

`authenticate` is a word Jackson has heard from his IT admin exactly once. `sign in` is what the button says. Match the button.

**Before (troubleshooting row):**
> Double-check your **credentials**. Use the eye icon to **verify your password**.

**After:**
> Double-check your **email and password**. Use the eye icon to **make sure your password is typed correctly**.

"Credentials" is a compliance/IT word. Jackson would say "my email and password" out loud.

---

## Page: Creating Your Account

No changes needed. This page was already written mostly in plain language:

> "Your institution's Landjourney account is set up during the onboarding process..."
> "1. An administrator at your institution adds you..."

The filter is not a mandate to change every page. Sometimes it just tells you "this one is fine."

---

## What this example teaches

Getting Started pages are short, but they're **first-impression pages**. The filter here is doing three jobs at once:

1. **Removing marketing vocabulary** (streamlines, empowering) that reads as promotional when Jackson is trying to sign in.
2. **Matching the app's own words** (sign in, not authenticate) so the docs and the button on screen agree.
3. **Cutting compound descriptions** ("toggle password visibility") down to what Jackson actually does ("show or hide your password").

Total surface change is small. But the pages that new users see first should get the tightest edit — because everything downstream depends on them not bouncing.
